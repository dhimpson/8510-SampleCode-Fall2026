THE HYGIENE OF PHYSICAL TRAINING.

WILLIAM H. BURNHAM, PH. D., -
CLARK UNIVERSITY, WORCESTER, MASS. |
II.

The second great problem is that of differentiation. What
should be the kind and extent of physical education for different
individuals under different conditions? This is a complex prob-
lem. It must be studied with regard to many relations. Among
the most important points are the following:

1. Physical education should be adapted to the individual.
The most serious injuries to health probably come from failure
to regard individual differences. Exercise within the limits of
health is entirely relative to the individual. People often ex-
claim at the foolhardy exploits of athletes; but whether a feat |
is foolish or not depends usually on the condition of training.
The difficult performance of the athlete in perfect training is
perhaps usually safer than running for an electric car by a man
whose muscles are not in training. And the most serious injury
is liable to come when an individual attempts at the outset what
others do after training, or tries to do after his muscles have
become flabby the things that he formerly did safely when in
training. Dr. Dukes (16, p. 76), for years physician to Rugby
School, says that “most accidents happen at the beginning of
each season.” Of training I wish to speak later under a special
rubric, but here it may be added that regard should be had not
merely to the individual’s condition of muscular development,
or even to the condition of the heart and lungs, but also to that
of the nervous system, and the general psycho-physic condition
as regards what we vaguely call vitality, power of endurance,

and the like. Hence the examination of each individual should
be broader than the mere testing of the muscular condition. .

Almost any rule, perhaps, that has ever been given in regard
to physical exercise or recreation is true enough for some indi-
vidual under certain conditions, and may be equally false for
many other individuals under different conditions.

The serious objection to athletics in the narrower sense is that
this form of exercise is suitable only for certain individuals fa-
vored by nature with special ability for athletic feats. For a
large part of the pupils of a school, as pointed out by an English
writer*, any considerable success in athletics is impossible, and
hence training of this sort is of relatively little value. On the
other hand, play has one great advantage over gymnastics. It
is more readily adapted to the individual. To gymnastics, when
not individualized, the words of Lagrange (45, pp. 813-815)
apply :

“It has the faults of its qualities; it perfects the man, but at
the expense of hard work of which not all men are capable; it
may form choice gymnasts, but it forms very few. ... . . Most
pupils are discouraged by the difficulties at the beginning, and
those who acquire a taste for it are those who are best endowed
physically, the strongest, or precisely those who can best do
without it. This select minority I admit acquire superior physi-
cal capacity, but weak subjects, or those of any medium strength,
find no benefit in the gymnastics, for the simple reason that they
do not practice it. . . . . Through all their life they have an aver-
sion to exercise, because exercise was presented to them in the
wrong way, under an arid and difficult form.”

2. It 1s of prime importance that training should be adapted
to the sequence of the stages of physiological development. In
a general ‘way this is recognized; but with our present inade-
quate knowledge of’ physical development, we cannot outline any
thoroughly scientific course. We do know, however, that there-
is a great difference between the physiological condition of the
child up to the age of puberty and the child after this period.
For example, the old measurements of Beneke (61, pp. 125,
126) indicate that during the period of development the volume
of the heart increases twelvefold, but the width of the aorta only
threefold; or in respect to a 100 cm. of height, in the case of the
child the heart volume is about 50, 1n the case of the adult 150
to 190 ccm. These measurements should be repeated with much
larger numbers ; but whether or not these figures are right, during
the school period there is a great change of the circulatory type. _

Probably there 1s a great difference also between the nervous

system of children and adolescents, and in the capacity or ability
. Of the nervous system for storing energy. For some reason not
well understood neuro-muscular energy does not seem to be
stored up by children in the same way that it is by boys and
girls after puberty. Roberts (59, pp. 367, 368), the English
anthropologist, applying this to athletic sport, says:

“With respect to the more active form of exercise it 1s certain
that football, cricket, and athletic contests, running, etc., do not
meet the requirements of young children. . . . . For some reason
not well understood, children under the age of twelve or thirteen
cannot be trained like older boys for athletic sports. The muscles
will not increase in size and harden in the limbs, in short, will
not ‘train.’ . . . . The physical work done, therefore, is the day’s
supply of energy and not the accumulated energy and adaptive-
ness of muscle associated with training, hence football soon ex-
hausts young children and is too violent while it lasts to be of
permanent advantage. The same objection applies to all forms
of violent exercise in the gymnasium or elsewhere, hence small
children enjoy playing games and are more benefited by them
than by athletics or gymnastics with apparatus. There are, how-
ever, many light gymnastic exercises with light dumb-bells, bars,
and clubs, suitable for young children of both sexes, and they ,
are frequently taught in schools.”

“After the age of thirteen years the body becomes capable of
training, and the sinews and muscles add to their energy from
day to day, provided the exercises are not carried on to the extent °
of producing fatigue and exhaustion. Athletic and active games,”
Roberts thinks, “are the most useful form of exercise after the
age of thirteen or fourteen. . . . . They should be made the cen-
tral feature of physical education to which all other forms should
be supplementary.”

As regards the storing of nervous energy in general, there is
probably a difference between the child and the adolescent, at
least between the child and the youth in the later stages of ado-
lescence. There are two theories in regard to the storing of
nervous energy; first, what may be called the reservoir theory,
that all the nervous energy is contained in one general store;
the other, the theory of a series of reservoirs, or that the nervous
energy of the organism 1s distributed, each important organ hav-
ing a special reservoir. According to the one theory, exhaustion
of any part exhausts the whole store of energy; according to the
other, exhaustion of any part may occur while other parts retain
their store of energy relatively undiminished. Now there is some
reason to believe that in the case of the child the theory of the
single reservoir represents the truth more nearly, while that of a

series of reservoirs represents the truth for the adolescent and the
adult. Thus it comes to pass that in adult life in case of over-
pressure the overworked organ itself is injured, while in case of
the child the organism adapts to the condition of overwork, draw-
ing upon the total store of energy in the body. Thus if the brain
is overworked there is likely to be overdevelopment of this organ
at the expense of the development of the heart, lungs, or the
skeletal structure.

On account of the great difference between the child and the -
adult and the child and the adolescent, it is imperative to adapt
the training to the sequence of the stages of development. A few
recent attempts at solving this problem practically are worth not-
ing. Among these is first of all the work of Tissié (64).

Tissié, starting from the fundamental principle that physical
exercise—like other forms of education—should be adapted to
the stage of development of the individual subject, distinguishes
four classes of exercises: 1, educative plays; 2, intensive plays;
3, sportive exercises ; 4, sports properly so called.

The “educative plays” are especially for young children up to
the age of twelve or thirteen years. They are the well-known
plays: running, jumping, walking, rolling the hoop, ball playing,
etc., and all exercises not mechanized and which do not require
too great effort for the child, or make too great demand on the
attention, or require prolonged immobility. Plays should be
recreative and should not stimulate to great nervous discharge.
One should respect nature, leave the child’s body to develop spon-
taneously by simple and easy exercises. All exercise which
greatly modifies respiration, or attitudes which act violently on the
muscles, which contract the chest, which compress the viscera,
which stretch the articulations too greatly, which modify the nor-
mal position, and stimulate the desire of surpassing one’s own
record or surpassing one’s companions, should be rejected in the
education of children. It is not well to exaggerate the movements
of the child by the use of special apparatus; one must take care
of the lungs and the heart as well as the intestines. Movements
ought never to congest the heart. No somersaults, no prolonged
suspension should be permitted. Gymnastics for children should
be medical and pedagogical. It is a time when one can change
bad postures, making the different muscular groups and articula-
tions function according to physiological plans.

While suspension from the fixed bar should be used sparingly,
Tissié emphatically condemns all apparatus for suspension from
an unstable equilibrium, such as trapeze, rings, and the like.
Some succeed, and one c6ees only the children who do succeed; but
one does not see those who have not continued the exercise, and

cially to the period from twelve or thirteen to sixteen. Such

etc., bicycling carefully regulated; but any extreme exercise

form movements rapidly, but short, never prolonged action is
permissible. Exercises on gymnastic apparatus should be em-
ployed physiologically. Every form of excess may have a detri- °
mental influence on the life. :

Third, the “sportive exercises” are especially for youth at the
period of adolescence, from sixteen to twenty. These comprise
all the sports in germ. Walking, mountain climbing, running,
leaping, bicycling, wrestling, riding, swimming, tug-of-war, etc.
Here one must regard not only the dynamic but the psychic func-
tions of each subject. At this period, from sixteen to twenty
years, exercises with apparatus can be permitted with more lati-
tude, but still within physiological limits. The aim should be
harmonious development of all parts of the body and the avoid-
ance of all acrobatic feats. .

Fourth, the “sports.” These are really for the later period of
adolescence or adult life.

In résumé of Tissié’s program, this classification corresponds to
the classification of the studies themselves according to the age
of each pupil. The “educative plays” are elemental training of
the muscles. The “intensive plays” are primary training. The
“sportive exercises” are secondary training. The “sports” are
the higher training. A good method cannot ignore well-estab-

lished facts in the physiology, psychology, and pathology of train-
ing. Man is not made for gymnastics, but gymnastics for man.

Perhaps the most successful attempt to devise a plan for physi-
cal training, adapted to age and physiological development, is that
made by Dr. Schmidt of Bonn and elaborated by Superintendent
Johnson of Pittsburg.* During the first period of school life,

_ from six to nine, a child’s exercise should be entirely in the form
of free play, and this out of doors as much as possible. Exer-
cises which involve, the large muscles and which stimulate respira-
tion and circulation are required. Such exercise is best given in

~ the form of children’s movement plays. During the next period,
from nine to twelve, the plays can be richer in content and greater
demands can be made ‘upon skill and speed. The various ball
plays, running, Jumping and the like, are in place. Gymnastic
exercises as such should have only a small place, amounting to
half an hour a day. The period from twelve to fifteen is char-.
acterized by the beginning of stronger growth of the lungs and
heart. Running exercises, swimming, skating, and the like, are
in place. The gymnastic exercises and the demands upon skill
and strength may be increased. Exercises with apparatus may
be given sparingly. During the period from fifteen to seventeen
the special characteristic is the great growth of heart in relation
to the arteries. Connected with this usually is greater increase
in height. Plays involving competition are in place,—association
football, cricket, basket ball, and the like. Exercises of speed
can be increased, but exercises requiring great strength, use of
heavy dumb-bells, and the like should still be avoided. During
the period from seventeen th twenty development is largely com-
pleted. All kinds of physical exercise can be increased and the
emphasis can be placed upon gymnastics with apparatus, exer-
cises of strength, sports, rowing, bicycling, and the like.

While perhaps open to criticism in some details, the main fea-
tures of such a program appear hygienic. But beyond a -few
general points like these, we cannot go. We have no data to:
show what system would be adapted to the sequence of the stages
of development. ,

With the chief attention centered upon muscular development,
as has been the rule in much of our gymnastic training thus far,
overtraining, or perhaps more strictly, improperly related devel-
opments, are liable to occur. Mosso (54) has pointed out this
danger. “Gymnastics,” he says, “are generally considered from
but one point of view, namely, their use in developing the muscles,
whilst their utility in respect to the organs is little thought of.”
With attention centered chiefly on muscular development, a form
of overtraining really injurious to some of the vital organs of
the body may result. If we go further and consider the effect
of physical training on the functional activity of the nervous sys-
tem and upon that extremely important but intangible factor that
we call vitality, power of endurance, of recuperation, or what
not, we have scarcely any data to show the effects of physical
training; but there are certain facts which indicate that in this
respect overtraining is liable to occur and a form of premature
development may result.

Muscular development as such is not evidence of health. A
man in complete training is said to be the worst case if disease
attack him. He lacks resistance, he lacks vitality, and the power
of recuperation. An English physician, Arabella Keneally,*
maintains that in case of women an extreme muscular develop-
ment is to be looked upon as evidence of disease. Mosso has also

The importance of physical exercise for girls is emphasized by
recent writers. Dr. Dukes (16, p. 79), for example, writes:

“Their education is almost inconceivably defective in principle.
It is only beginning to be realized that girls require and deserve
the same care and thought that are bestowed upon the education
and training of boys. The great defect in most schools for girls
is the utter neglect of physical education; whereas it should pos-
sess a prior claim to their intellectual education if we are to attain
the development of strong, active, healthy, vigorous women; and
yet no effort 1s made to produce them, during the only years in
which they can be effected, the years of active growth and devel-
opment. Taking note of the suggestions of physiology, in the
case of girls exercise is even more imperative for their proper
development than for boys. It is the absence of daily regular,
and sufficient exercise which renders girls listless, apathetic, and
anzmic; it accounts for their constipation ; and is answerable for
their crooked backs, knock-knees, and flat feet with characteristic
awkward gait. A better physical education would also control
more effectively than any other mode the emotions of girls which
are so apt to impede the production of a symmetric nature.”

THE RELATION OF SCHOOL HYGIENE TO PHYSICAL
EDUCATION.*
THOMAS A. STOREY, M. D., PH. D.,
ASSOCIATE PROFESSOR OF PHYSICAL INSTRUCTION IN THE COLLEGE
OF THE CITY OF NEW YORK.
The objects of school hygiene and the objects of physical edu-
. cation are, in their final analysis, identical. Both lines of work
are developed for the ultimate purpose of turning out healthy,
vigorous and effitient young men and women. Neither is con-
tent with a product that is merely well—it must be actively and
aggressively well. “Health” does not mean merely an absence
of disease. It means organic vigor and organic efficiency so
that in the combat against disease there is no armistice, no truce,
but a decisive victory in favor of the well-equipped, well-trained,
and well-defended organism.

School hygiene and physical education in the school or college
are then necessarily concerned with all those influences which
affect the health of school children and college students. These
influences reside in the school environment of the individual; in |
the school habits of the individual; in the physical condition of
the individual, and in the health instruction which he receives.

It is obvious that the responsibility of the school for the
character of these influences rests on the one hand with the
governing body of the institution, which 1s called the school
board, or board of education, or board of trustees, and on
the other hand with some one or more of the regularly
organized departments of the school. Logically these depart-
ments are most likely to be either the department of hygiene,

- or the department of physical education. The efficiency of
either of these departments rests largely upon the success
with which the governing body of the institution has met
its peculiar responsibility in matters related to the health of
its pupils or students. If these departments are restricted by
unfortunate school conditions or limited by inadequate support,
their influence on the individual must be correspondingly in-
efficient. Both of these departments look to the institutional
authorities for the same sort of support. In giving this support
those authorities are only filling an obligation which they owe

to society. Every institution of organized society which brings
numbers of individuals together and places them under a peculiar
environment for a period of several hours each day, and con-
tinues this influence for a period of years, is under a serious ob-
ligation to make that environment safe, sanitary and healthful.
In meeting this obligation the school or college is furnishing the
department of physical education or the department of school
hygiene with a basis on which it may accomplish effective
work. These obligations of the school board or trustees may be
outlined as follows: First, they must support the sanitary and
hygienic obligations involved in selecting the site, architecture
and material equipment of their buildings, and in the organization
of their administration. These obligations make it incumbent on
the authorities to locate these buildings in the most desirable
neighborhood available. The factors that make location desir-
able from ‘the standpoint of health are found in the facilities
offered for drainage, the exposure to sunshine and fresh air, the
protection from or absence of wind, dust, and smoke, the pres-
ence of playgrounds, and the sanitary and moral character of
the neighborhood. These obligations further necessitate the
‘ adoption of a style of architecture suited to the needs of the
numbers of children or students who will use the conveniences
provided. There must be an abundance of light, sufficient heat,
adequate ventilation, good water supply, modern plumbing, and
adequate cleaning facilities. The character and location of toilet
rooms, drinking founts, lockers, desks, and blackboards are mat-
ters of great importance in this connection. Then the school or
college authorities are under obligation to provide a capable and
efficient administrative machinery, and this obligation necessi-
tates a selection of teachers and other employees whose physical
condition is in no way a menace to the health of others. These
employees must not only be capable and efficient, they must in — -
addition be hygienically and healthfully safe, not only in theory
but also in practice.

This responsibility for the status of those influences which
affect the health of young people in school or college, further
makes it incumbent upon the authorities to protect these young
people from those of their comrades who may be carriers of dis-
ease. To support this obligation, the authorities must institute
and enforce a careful, systematic, and adequate plan of medical,
hygienic and sanitary supervision, and must back up that super--
vision with an authority to condemn and remove all sources of
medical, sanitary or hygienic danger, so that no individual may
be said to have lost his good health because of adverse institu-
tional conditions.

And finally, these authorities are under obligation to so arrange
the school and collegiate schedule as to respect the laws of health.
The schedule should respect the needs of the individual for .
recreation, for food and for unhurried eating, for the care of his
excretions, for exercise, for bathing, and for theoretical and
practical instruction concerning the fundamental laws of health. |
It must be apparent that in filling these obligations which the
institution owes to organized society, the school authorities are
furnishing a necessary basis for the proper development of either
a department of physical education or a department of school
hygiene. The work of either of these departments is limited in
efficiency whenever and wherever the board of trustees has failed
in any one respect to support its obligation to protect the health
of those individuals that are brought under the influence of the
institution. The efforts of school hygiene or physical education
will not reach very far in the face of ten or twelve years of bad
recitation-room ventilation, regardless of the completeness of the .
departmental organization and policy in other respects. A fifteen
minute lunch throughout a high school course will spoil a pupil’s
digestion in spite of his knowledge of the nature and functions
of his organs of alimentation. The school authorities may pro-
vide adequate ventilation, good light, and an efficient cleaning
system, andethe department of physical education or the depart-
ment of school hygiene may teach the principles and habituate in-
dividuals to the practice of wise health procedures, but these de-
partments will fail to achieve the best health influences if the
board has neglected to authorize, enforce and support an adequate
system of medical, hygienic and sanitary supervision. No matter
how complete the organization may be, either as a department of
physical education or as a department of hygiene, the departmental
success in matters of health will be limited in proportion to the
degree in which the school authorities have met their obligations.
It is obvious then, that school hygiene and physical education
are dependent on the school or collegiate authorities for support
along identical lines. This, coupled with the fact that the objects
of these departments are identical, indicates a very close relation-
ship.
I have pointed out the fact that school hygiene and physical
. education have the same objects in view, and that each is depend-
ent upon the school authorities for support along identical lines.
Permit me now to call your attention to the similarity of work
which these two departments must develop in order to realize
their ideals.
It will be admitted without debate that any department which
is calculated to secure and conserve the health of school children

must be successful along the following lines, it being assumed that
the school authorities have fully supported their own peculiar
health responsibilities.

First: There must be an expert, capable and effective medical,
sanitary and hygienic supervision of the entire institution; of all

_ its subdivisions ; of all its fixtures, and of all its attending mem-
bers. This supervision must be a supervision with authority to
enforce its decisions. Through it, all sanitary deficiencies and
difficulties may be corrected and removed; the personal hygiene
of attendants, pupils and teachers improved; and an effective
system of medical examination, inspection, and consultation estab-
lished. The medical supervision should have power to debar from
the institution all cases of contagion, all cases whose condition
would be a menace to the health, hygiene or comfort of others ;.
it should have power to require the parent to provide his child
with proper treatment for his remediable physical defects, not
only for the benefit of the child, but also for the benefit of his
companions and for the future benefit of society.

The sanitary supervision should include among other things,
regular examinations of the premises (this may mean the roof),
the school neighborhood, recitation rooms, hallways, corridors,
stairs, offices, toilet rooms, sinks, bathing facilities, locker rooms,
water supply, drainage and cleaning system. .

The hygienic supervision should include a regular inquiry into
the bodily cleanliness of the individual, the condition of his
clothing, his exercising uniform and towel, if he has any; his
habits of bathing, eating, drinking, and care of his excretions.
This supervision is more or less inseparable from medical super-
vision when the latter is in operation. .

The medical supervision includes, first, a regular and frequent
inspection of the skin and hair, eyes, ears, nose, mouth and
throat; second, a medical consultation whenever an individual
‘returns to work after illness; whenever he feels unwell; and
whenever his instructor notes evidences of abnormality; third,
a careful and thorough medical examination of each individual
on entering the school and at appropriate intervals during his
stay there. This examination should cover the skin and hair,
eyes, ears, nose, mouth, throat, heart and circulation, lungs,
abdomen, genito-urinary organs, bones, joints, and nervous
system.

Second, in addition to the exercise of such a medical, hygienic
and sanitary supervision, the school department calculated to
influence the health of school children or college students must
offer instruction concerning the simple fundamental laws of
human health. This instruction should be pedagogically applied

and it must be scientifically correct. It should cover such sub-
jects as the following: first, the ways and means of securing and
conserving health. It should teach in this connection, the simple
and essential facts of nutrition and how to eat; care of the ex-
cretions ; exercise; rest and recreation; breathing; bathing and
bodily cleanliness. Second, it should teach the main facts con-
nected with the influence of abnormal bodily conditions and
habits on health, covering such conditions as defective vision,
obstructed breathing, bad teeth, spinal curvature, flat feet, and
constipation; and such habits as mouth breathing, rapid eating,
incomplete mastication, and the use of tea, coffee, alcohol and
tobacco. Third, it should give instruction concerning the various
common causes of disease, such as bacteria, the malarial para-
site, the spirochete pallida, pediculus, ring worm, and the acarus
scabiei. It should impart an accurate knowledge of the larger
details concerning the size, shape, habitat, habits, development
and: disease influences of these parasites. Fourth, this instruc-
tion should acquaint the child with our various sommon import-
ant carriers of disease, such as the malarial mosquito, the typhoid
fly, the human being who spits; dirty milk, water and other con-
taminated food; the cat, dog, head louse, flea, and bedbug; with
dust and dirt, filth and so on. Fifth, this instruction should
convey information concerning our available defenses against
disease. It should cover such defenses as isolation of contagious
cases; fumigation; cleanliness of body, clothing and surround-
ings; the influence of sunshine and fresh air; and the value of
immunity. It should teach that health is our best defense against
disease, and that health depends pretty largely on right living
from day to day. Sixth, and finally, this instruction concerning
the simple laws of health should acquaint the individual with the
main facts of our common diseases. Every high school child,
and assuredly every college graduate, should know the impor-
tant facts of tuberculosis, typhoid fever, smallpox, pnuemonia,
malaria, measles, diphtheria, syphilis, gonorrhoea, the bubonic
plague, cholera, yellow fever, a bad cold, tonsilitis, acute rheu-
matism, appendicitis, nephritis, hydrophobia and so on. A gen-
eral accurate knowledge of the main facts of these common dis-
eases would do much to reduce their prevalence.

I cannot claim to have covered all the subjects that should be
taught in this connection, but you must admit that an ignorance
of any one of these subjects may easily be and often seriously
is the cause for the health failure of a human life. An ignorance
of the cause of a certain disease, an ignorance of its mode of
transmission or dissemination, an ignorance of our defenses
against it, or an ignorance of its nature, is an ignorance that may

be fatal. In the face of such ignorance our efforts for health
are often futile.

I‘inally, a department organized for the purpose of turning
out healthy, vigorous young men and young women must give
wise, careful and practical instruction in health habits, ‘such as
physical exercise; breathing, bodily cleanliness, bathing, and care
of personal surroundings. Here should be brought into opera-
tion the vast resources of supervised physical exercise, in doors
and out doors. Drills, apparatus work, games, sports and play,
swimming, running, jumping and vaulting should be combined
and organized in the interest of the health and character develop-
ment of the individual. This is one of the most important di-
visions of work necessary to the success of a department organ-
ized for the purpose of teaching children or adults the ways and
means of securing and conserving health. :

Such a department, then, should be developed along the follow-

ing lines:

First, it should exercise an efficient medical, sanitary and
hygienic supervision over the school, its equipment, employees,
instructors and pupils or students.

Second, it should offer instruction concerning the simple laws
of human health, and

Third, it should administer practical instruction in health
habits. with particular reference to physical exercise.

It must employ all legitimate and practical means that will
help to equip young men and young women with a knowledge
of the ways and means of securing and conserving health. It

: must utilize every available and practical method of habituating
young men and young women to the practice of those ordinary
everyday health procedures which are essential to right living.
It must exercise with power such sanitary, hygienic, and medical
supervision as will protect the individual from his own adverse
health influences as well as from the adverse health influences
of his school environment.

If this department neglects any of these lines of work it can-
not hope for full success even though its organization is in other
respects perfect. No department can secure proper health re-

sults in an individual whose mouth 1s full of decayed teeth. The
sum total of benefit which a mouth breather will draw from such
a department will be limited until he breathes regularly through
his nose. The department that neglects to secure, through medi-
cal supervision, a correction of such defects and habits will fail
to achieve the health influences for which it was organized. This
type of failure can be avoided only through an adequate medical,
sanitary and hygienic supervision.

And, again, ignorance of the laws of health will invite disaster.
The young man that sleeps in a closed room will not benefit
greatly from his other habits however good they may be. Ina
certain great university a student lost his eyesight because he
washed his sore eyes with recently voided urine. He was igno-
rant, and, although a college student, he followed advice the sort
and source of which he could not have respected had he received
previously some intelligent instruction concerning the cause and :
dissemination of disease. Such advice is given more frequently
than you think. Many of our recently added foreign children
are taught at home that head-lice are a sign of strength; some
of them are taught to apply butter and raw potatoes to fresh
wounds; some are habituated to the practice of sewing on the
underclothing at the beginning of winter and not changing until
spring ; and many are not taught to wash their hands before eat-
ing. Such ignorance is a type that can be removed only through
intelligent education concerning the laws of health. Ignorance
of these laws will make successful efforts for the acquisition and
conservation of health futile, regardless of the excellence of the
other procedures adopted.

An individual may learn hygienic precepts; he may have a
recitation room knowledge of bacteria, their habits and their re-
lation to disease, but he may be flabby, weak, and non-resistant
because he has never put his health precepts into practice. If
he does not eat right; or 1f he does not exercise wisely; or if he
does not look after his excretions ; or if he does not rest properly ;

or if he is not reasonably clean, he cannot secure the health re-
sults that are necessary for his best welfare.

Let me repeat: If this department, organized for the purpose

_ of turning out healthy, vigorous, efficient young men and young
women, fails to develop along any one of these important lines,
it cannot succeed in accomplishing the objects for which it is
organized.

And now let me ask a few questions: If this department
which I have discussed were called the department of school
hygiene, what part or parts of the work which I have outlined
would be left out? If this department were called the depart-
ment of physical education, what part or parts of the work which
I have outlined would be left out?

If school hygiene limits itself to instruction concerning the
care of the teeth, and the evil effects of alcohol, its effects upon
the health of the human race will be infinitesimal. If physical
education contents itself with mass drills and apparatus work,
its effects on the vigor of the nation will be microscopic. The
time has passed when we can be content to teach hygiene out of

a text book and let it go at that. The time has passed when we
can make a few measurements and administer a routine drill and
call our work “physical education.” Such limitations can no
longer exist. The fields of these two lines of work have broad-
ened and overlapped until today they coincide. There can be
no complete scheme of school hygiene without physical education,
and there can be no complete scheme of physical education with-
out school hygiene. They have the same serious and important
objects ; their adequate support demands of the school authorities
the fulfilment of identical obligations, the burden of which is
placed there by organized society; their methods must be the
same; and the content of their courses must be the same.

The new constitution proved on the whole the most satisfactory
of any as yet adopted but there was more work yet to be done
before it reached its present form. <A majority vote of.the Coun-

many points, and to have the election depend upon the number
of points. This called forth a storm of protest from the Boston
society.

At a meeting of the Council, December 13, 1905, amendments
to the constitution were presented to the following effect:

That the Council should hold an annual meeting, at which time
it should elect officers, receive formal reports from societies,
secretary, editor, and treasurer. That no amendments to the con-
stitution should take effect until they had been approved by a two-
thirds vote at an annual meeting of the Council. That such
amendments must have been approved by a preceding meeting of
the Council or executive committee and published in the REVIEw
not less than three months previous to the annual meeting of the
Council. That all power to rescind the votes of the Council in
any particular be taken from the convention, and all business be
left in the hands of the Council with the exception of the election
of honorary members.

America in its August Proceedings, is going to mark an epoch
in the development of the playgrounds and perhaps of physical
training in this country. I do not say this because the report
itself is faultless or that it will survive its present form; but I am
convinced that whatever may be its faults, it has arranged a large
body of new material, and it has demonstrated that the play-
grounds may give a real system of education. “It 1s a body of
material that educators cannot hereafter neglect, physical educa-
tors least of all.

The first course outlined in the report 1s called a ‘Course for
Professional Directors.” The presupposition that lies back of
this is naturally that the day of the professional director has ar-
rived. We have been temporizing with the play movement up
to the present time, organizing our systems each summer in many
cities to disband them again in the fall; renewing each summer
the process of the training of workers, only to lose all the results .
of the training with the loss of the worker by her going on a
vacation in the middle of the summer or going back to her school
at the end of it. All the organization and the spirit of the chil-
dren laboriously built up during the summer has been scattered
to the winds in the unorganized play of the rest of the year.

> The whole tendency of growth is toward permanency of organi-
zation and positions. The municipal playgrounds in our larger ‘
cities are coming to be open all the year round, in charge of em-
ployees who hold their positions for the year. There is constant -
growth of the movement for field houses, public baths with
gymnasiums attached, and municipal gymnasiums. The play-
ground season 1s steadily lengthening in the smaller cities, and we
may expect soon to see these positions also held for the year.

Ordinarily under the conditions of the open air playground,
which is not lighted at night, it becomes almost necessary to close
the playgrounds as soon as school begins, for the reason that the
small part of the day which is not used by the school is too short -
and murky for playground purposes. It is scarcely worth the
effort to open the playgrounds at half past three, and to close
them again at half past four.

If the playgrounds are opened at three o’clock in the afternoon

What 1s there that stands in the way? The mothers want

‘The amateur problem is one of those discussions that is always
with us. It began with the dawn of athletic rivalry between
groups. It existed among those people we are wont to think

, ideal in sports as in art. It arose with the rise of sports in Eng-

American athletics. :

Our present situation is the result of social conditions. With
the rapid rise of American athletics without traditions to guide
us we have gone on many by-paths of athletic adventure. Neg-
lect in proper leadership brought on corruption, which authorities

met with drastic legislation. Those hit, countered with a voluble

attack on the traditional ideals of amateur sport. Having no

deeply rooted public opinion on athletics as an educational and

social force, there has developed through lack of information

and insight a widespread doubt of the wisdom of amateurism.

- In the attack, Anglophobia has taken its part. Even intellectual

paders have been heard to say that the amateur law came from

ngland, from entirely different social conditions, that it had

. no place in American democratic life, etc., without once paus-

ing to consider whether the law had any feundation in human

needs or any necessary significance in human relationships. It

is surprising the number of college men that have dropped like

plums into the apron of the opposition over the college summer

baseball controversy, because the arguments are seductive and

hard to meet by the layman and because we have no strong back-
ground of national ideals in play or sport.

We need some constructive criticism. Amateurism can no
longer stand on its traditional legs. Public faith in its justice
is tottering. We must either modify our law or show that ama-
teurism is a necessary element fn human playful relationships. |
If amateurism wins public confidence, it must be demonstrated
that the law is rooted in human nature and social forces, and
essential for a healthful boy’s play life. To consider this phase
of the discussion will be the purpose of this paper.

In considering these points, I shall assume that we are all
agreed on the functions and values of athletic sports for boys,
i. e. the values for organic development, motor development and
character discipline, that all recognize athletics as the natural
physical-social education of boys, that they are especially impor-
tant in the later adolescent years, and that we want all, fitted
physically, to take part in them. As educators and social work-

We shall first analyze the primary impulse, pleasure, interest,
motive, that leads to participation in play or athletics; then the
impulses that create the professional.

At the foundation of all motor play there is a satisfaction in
the motor discharge exhibited by the young of all animals; a sat-
isfaction of the primitive hunger for activity, together with the
sense stimuli which are intensely pleasureable. Closely asso-
ciated are other sensuously agreeable stimuli most strikingly
illustrated in adult life by the pleasure in watching a thrown
object. |

To these fundamental pleasures in play there are added a long
series of pleasurable emotional states appearing more or less in
all motor plays from infancy to adulthood. There is the conflict
of daring and fear in feats, the pleasure in accomplishment and
success, the pleasure and pride in overcoming difficulties and en-
countering risk or danger with all its emotional tension, the ex-
altation that comes in the rebound from fear through relief, the
tension of expectation and shock of surprise, the pleasure of en-
during hardships and suppressing pain, the pleasure in the feeling
of mastery of self, the inspiration of being a cause, and all the
emotional content ‘that holds attention and heightens the reality
of life which 1s opposed to ennui and which for the adolescent
is a neurological necessity.

Then there are the impulses which influence the form of play.
The combative, social and egoistic impulses, appearing in play
from early childhood, become especially prominent with adoles-
cence. Simple running for its own sake soon loses its charm and
must be turned into a contest, thus satisfying the combative im-
pulse. Rolling about on the floor is changed into a tussle. De-
structiveness is sometimes prominent. Through all childhood
there is intense pleasure in being chased and chasing, hiding,
being sought and seeking. .

The egoistic impulse combines with the combative to give keen-
ness to do something as well as, or better, than some one else.
This tendency becomes peculiarly strong in the adolescent period.
So out of rivalry we derive all the fighting games and contests of
youth. These are perpetuated and emphasized by imitation.

social superiority. The boy who will fight with a superior is
considered unusually gritty. A boy that will not fight among his
equals when the code demands it 1s considered a coward. A boy
who fights only his inferiors is a coward and a bully. The code
requires a boy to fight within his own class. This 1s the law of
competition.

A sharp distinction must be made between play, the issue of
which is not serious, and play charged with rivalry. The law
of competition operates in proportion to the seriousness of the
contest as a test of powers and therefore is particularly important
in our discussion.

There is often play at a contest where a serious contest could
not exist.

A boy of six, one of eight, a girl of nine, two young ladies, a
judge, a physician, a banker and his wife, a college professor and
his wife played ‘baseball’ on the front lawn. For the boys it
was a contest; for the adults it was a rollicking good time with
laughter in proportion to the comic elements present. The judge
suddenly exhibited rivalry and lined out a ball which passed just
over the head of the six-year-old boy on second base. Instantly
the play vanished. The boy wiggled, looked disappointed and
mumbled, “pshaw, that isn’t fair.” The parents realized the

574 AMERICAN PHYSICAL EDUCATION REVIEW
“close call” and tactfully changed the entertainment to refresh-
ments. The game was play until rivalry and skill came in.
Serious competition could not exist in such groups.
A boy will tussle with his father with all his energy or a girl
again will wrestle with her older brother, but should the father
or the brother use more than just enough power to neutralize the
efforts of their struggling antagonist and so keep the struggle
interesting, or exhibit rivalry and use the tactics they would use
against equals, immediately there comes the complaint, “Oh,
you're rough.” Either there must be reform or the play ceases.
Here again there is play at a contest where there could be no
_ contest. Similarly a young lady or an inexperienced gentleman
will play at a contest in tennis or golf with a champion but they
will refuse to compete against him. |
Often real contests take place between individuals or teams
that are unequal in strength, but the inequality is recognized and
an inequality in score expected. Inexperienced individuals,
, ambitious to learn, often enter the inconspicuous preliminaries
of tennis or golf tournaments. High school football teams often
play against superior college teams, and small college teams
against great university teams. Rivalry enters and the score is
"a serious item, but the balance of the score is recognized before- ;
hand or there could be no contest. The weaker team does its
best, the stronger sometimes does also, but the outcome is pre-
ordained and there is no disturbance of social feelings. Such
contests can exist so long as the spectators and the public recog- |
nize the natural inequality and make allowance for it in the score. |
Unrecognized, the contest would not exist, because of the work- ,
ings of the law of competition. :
In contests between individuals or teams that are supposed to |
be equals the law of competition has full sway. The law is em- |
phasized by the rivalry of partisan backers. The desire for fair-
ness in an equal opportunity becomes insistent. The sharper the |
rivalry, the more closely are the lines drawn. Nowhere in life |
does the demand for a square deal so promptly assert itself. Un-
fairness ruins the contest.
Here we come to the core of our problem. We are concerned
chiefly with the upper end of play, the contest, in the later ado-
lescent years, when rivalry is most keen. This is the contesting
age. It is here that the law of competition becomes peculiarly
important in determining any classification for participation.
This brings us naturally to the last division of our analysis and
the ultimate question in the amateur discussion. What place and
influence has the professional motive among the factors control-
ling participation? To put it another way: What effect has the

professional motive on participation when brought in contact
with the playful or amateur motive?

The effect of the pure or regular adult professional is seen at
a glance, is recognized by all and gives no cause for discussion.
He eliminates the amateur under the law of competition. Given
partisans with money and no amateur regulation and he would
eliminate the amateur from all highly organized contests repre-
senting a group. The extent of this elimination, if allowed,
down the years into boy life, is limited only by the living wage ~
and the financial capacity of partisans. If we are to have highly
organized contests to satisfy the amateur motive it is clear that
pure professional activities must be kept separated from the
amateur by an effective law of classification. Over this there is
no dispute.

Our difficulty is not with the pure professional but with the
petty professional who has irregular or intermittent professional
experience. The money motive in the petty professional is linked
with desires for a little extra spending money, a trip, a pleasant
summer outing or a means of making expenses in school, etc.
Our first interest 1n these experiences is not in the moral nature
of the acts themselves but in the results which they have on par-
ticipation in contests when the actor is brought in contact with
the pure play motive. The mcaning of play an boy life demands
that the amatcur motive and the participation of the majority be |
conserved. .

It is clear that professional experience makes more expert per-
formers. Practice gives skill; practice among the devotees of an.
art gives superior knowledge. No one denies this; it is one of

- the stock arguments used by those opposing the amateur law.
Other things being equal the boy with professional experience
will always have greater chances of making a place on the team
of his group than the boy who has had no professional experience.
This means that among the typical mass of boys the profession-
ally experienced is a special or exceptional case. Against him in -
the contest the typical boy is handicapped. He is defeated; the
professionally experienced wins. The two parties are not equally
matched ; the contest is unfair. By the law of competition the
typical boy is deprived of competition. This undemocratic in-
fluence alone establishes the place of the money motive as repre-
sented by the petty professional among the influences controlling
the participation of the many. and explains the necessity of a law
fixing a classification, separating the money motive from the
amateur. "

But the results do not stop with the elimination of the majority.
This is only the first step. A little experience in athletics and

contact with the petty professional, teaches the typical boy inter-
ested in athletics why he is handicapped. He is outclassed be-
cause of his competitor’s superior experience. If his athletic
impulse is strong and he’craves to make the highest team of his
group the incentives to gain skill by the methods of his competi-
tors will be strong. He is denied the pleasure of participation
until he gains superior skill either by a large expenditure of time
or by professional experience. He must accept defeat, or do as
the Romans do.

In this connection there are two points to be noted. First, the
time element controls those with the higher ambitions even if they
have no scruples against professional experience. To the
ambitious, time is valuable; a disproportionate amount of time
cannot be spent on things purely physical and recreative. Among
college men the influences here are striking. College athletes,
even under present conditions, have little time free from routine
studies and athletics. They must forego other recreative and
culture activities. Ambitious men refuse to make the sacrifice.
They tend to withdraw from athletic participation in proportion
as the administration disregards their higher interests. They not
only lose the benefits of athletics but athletics lose the influence
of them. What these boys want 1s not money but their athletic
rights. They have a right to the education of athletics without
a loss of culture life. They have a right to enter an athletic or-
ganization that gives the hope of achieving athletic honors without
sacrificing their higher ambitions. A custom that etther dts-
courages general culture or the education contained 1n athletics
must be condemned. In so far as the petty professional tends
to create a situation requiring an excessive expenditure of time
in sports, he must be legislated ont of existence.

The second point concerns the generally unwholesome in-
fluences of professional experience. Making an exhibition of
one’s self for money before a miscellaneous crowd of pleasure
seekers who supply the money is not calculated to develop the
best type of character or intelligence for American citizenship.
The appeal is to impulses low in the scale of character and social
values. However much professional sport may be elevated in
essential elements, 1t will-always remain low in the scale of human
occupations. The trend of its influence 1s low. Manners and
customs, especially the gambling, language, and humor, are not
refining. There 1s plenty of time for loafing, usually with sporty
associations. The occupation and environment emphasize the
sporting news habit. The sporting page, the most licensed and
unwholesome. page in the modern newspaper, 1s the literature of
the professional. An experienced professional once said that it

took only about four years of professional summer baseball to
make a man “nutty.”

While there are many “good fellows” and many intelligent men
in professional athletics, this does not alter the status of the oc-
cupation nor the trend of its influences. It is not the atmosphere
in which educators and social workers wish boys to be educated.
Earnest parents, as they see the growing importance of athletics
in the lives of their boys and realize the moral influences of pro-
fessionalism, will insist that their boys be guarded from profes-
sional experience. Boys without earnest parents must also be
protected. Therefore educators and social workers must con-
demn professional experience for the typical mass of boys. In-
asmuch as the money motive breeds the money motive, and pro-
fessionalism breeds professional ideals, we must legislate against
both.

As a final product of all these tendencies the status of athletics
as play is lowered in public esteem. Among the serious classes
social sentiments develop that are dangerous to the physical and
social welfare of boys. The boy of good social training conceives
of athletics as something foreign to his life and education. Ath-
letics are for those other fellows, the motor specialist, the mate-
rial. Ile tends to lose all sense of the meaning of athletics for
himself. The public tends to lose sight of the distinction between
athletics as a spectacle for the pleasure of the spectator and ath-
letics as play for the pleasure and education of the boy.

It is clear, therefore, that if we are to have any highly organ-
ized interesting athletics that are strong in wholesome rivalry and
that will appeal to the average intelligent, self-respecting boy in
the later adolescent period, and also command public esteem, the
athlete with the money motive must be eliminated from the con-
test with the athlete with the amateur motive. This we have
shown can be done only by a classification that separates sharply
the money or professional motive from the play or amateur
motive. This classification is the law of amateurism.

It has been our purpose to search for the roots of amateurism
in human nature and the need of an amateur law in social forces.
We have shown that amateurism is a eoncept of the motives in
play which is as necessary to the life of youth as air. We have
shown that a law of amateurism 1s necessary 1f athletics, as the
play of the later adolescent years, is to achieve its biological and
social ends.

There are many moral problems, many questions concerning
the nature of the law, and many administrative difficulties which
we have not touched. We have been after the foundations of
amateurism, and have dealt only with ultimate problems to which

all arguments must resort in final appeal. What we need 1s con-
viction of the soundness of the law.

In closing we might call attention to the fact that amateurism
as a principle and as a law in the administration of athletics is
democratic. It aims to conserve the natural rights of the many
as against the privileges of the few. The selfish seekers after
personal profit, in addition to social applause through pleasant
and exciting activities, will always oppose it. Thoughtless and
short-sighted partisans who gain a cheap pleasure from the selfish
privilege seeker will also oppose the law. The amateur law and
the administration of the law must seek to eliminate the few
seekers after special privilege for the benefit of the many. Ad-
ministrators must teach the public that professional athletics are
for the pleasure of the spectator and that amateur athletics are
for the pleasure and benefit of the participant. |

Pure AIR IN THE GYMNASIUM.

During October most of the gymnasium classes in the Young
Men’s Chrisian Association, clubs and public schools have been
organized for the winter. The college and academy classes will
all be running by the end of November.

Pure air is the first essential in making the gymnasium building
a hall of health. Purity of air means chiefly absence of dust and
odors, abundance of oxygen and the minimum of carbon dioxide.
The feather duster and broom are no longer used to stir up the
dust in well regulated gymnasiums. The kerosene fed brooms
and other dust laying devices and preparations serve to keep down
the dust. The damp cloth instead of the duster has worked great
improvements. The vacuum cleaners should take the place of
broom and duster. Satisfactory stationary and portable systems
are now manufactured which will keep the gymnasium mats and
locker rooms free from dust.

Purity of air from the- gymnasium standpoint must reckon
with the fact that a man doing active work vitiates the air from
five to nine times as rapidly as he would at rest. Thorough venti-
lation under these circumstances means that 150 to 270 cubic feet
of fresh air per minute should be brought into the gymnasium
for each individual. The ordinary change of air which occurs

once an hour through the walls and around the windows will not
suffice for these needs. Much may be accomplished by a thor-
ough airing through the opening of windows of the gymnasium
_ and locker rooms in the morning before classes. Adequate ven-
tilation cannot ordinarily be secured without some auxiliary to
natural ventilation in the form of heated air shafts or vacuum

or penum fans.

The problem of supplying sufficient fresh air to gymnasiums
has not been satisfactorily settled. In but few gymnasiums is
the air tested as to its temperature, purity or relative humidity.
An examination of the air in the gymnasium may be as impor-
tant a procedure concerning the health of the pupil as the physi-
cal examination given in the office.

SURFACE ANATOMY FOR STUDENTS OF PHYSICAL TRAINING, by .

J.C. Bridge, M. R. C. S., L. R. C. P., D. P. H., Medical
Officer to the Carnegie Dunfermline Trust, Medical Inspector
of School Children, Lecturer at the Colleges of Hygiene and
Physical Training, Dunfermline. Published by R. K. Lind-
say & Co., The Abbey Press, 1909, Dunfermline. Price, one
shilling per part. Part I., Bones. Part II., Muscles.

The author offers a brief outline of the surface markings of the
body as a partial substitute for dissection for those students of
physical training who cannot secure it. Directions are given for
marking out bones and muscles on the living subject with the
ordinary skin pencil. The notes are written entirely from the
viewpoint of those interested in physical training and do not
mention points that concern only the medical and surgical stu- °
dent. Part I. errs in being too brief. Otherwise the notes will
be found very helpful in centering the attention of the student
upon the living ‘body instead of the text book.—E. B.
