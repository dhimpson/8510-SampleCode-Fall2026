The Epidemic of Lynching in the South
Monthly Oration Delivered Before the Philosophian Literary
Society by Carroll S. Maree

Once the name of the South was synonymous with purity
and honor, and the highest type of a gentleman was the South-
ern cavalier. What were the foremost characteristics of Gen.
Robert E. Lee? They were his sincere religion and his quiet,
gentlemanly manner toward all men. And so it was with
Jackson, Stuart and Hampton, and with all the Southern
heroes of the Civil War. No Chesterfield or courtier of Louis
XIV could have excelled the Southern gentleman in politeness
or in knightly grace. The murderer, the debauchee, and the
libertine were put under the ban by society. The war might
sweep away the Southerner’s fortune; the gloomiest adversity
might come, but he could not be made any less a gentleman.
The South was then the home of the true and the brave.

a man takes his life in his hands when traveling through the
South. Is their conception of us a wholly false one? No; a
thousand times no! We have given the world a right to think
thus and should not be afraid to confess it. The bird of free-
dom has been encaged by the Southern lyncher, and there is a
stain on the escutcheon of the South. The section which pro-
duced Calhoun and Andrew Jackson is now the object of scorn
and ridicule. Since the Civil War the epidemic of lynching
has been constantly growing in the South. Beginning with
the Ku-Klux Klan of Reconstruction times and extending down
to the present, it has had a career of crime, of shame, and of
disgrace.

But some one will say that lynchings occur
Taking the statistics of the cases of mob-murder compiled for
the year 1892 we find that 241 persons were executed by lynch-
ers in the United States during that year, all of these cases ex-
cept thirty-eight occurring in the South, and 160 of that num-
ber of victims being negroes. I think these statistics are con-
vincing.

Let us stop and consider the magnitude of this crime for
a few moments. A mob of crazed men, with usually a skillful
and designing leader at their head, take it into their hands to
mete out justice to an offender, without having held any form
of trial whatever. Where could we find any one with less
judgment than an infuriated man? Last April a negro was
burned by a mob, and later developments showed that he was
entirely guiltless of the charge which had been made against
him. Such is very often the case. The very angels cluster-
ing around God’s immaculate throne moan to see such a crime
as this.
And then, for what a small offence is a man thus some-
times brutally murdered! There are four cases on record in
which the charge was robbery. In 1903 a negro was lynched
for breaking a white man’s arm. Anglo-Saxon, is this your
civilization—your boasted humanity?

Gentlemen, to strike a little nearer home, even here in
South Carolina we have had the disgraceful affair of Lake
City, together with many others. South Carolina, the first
State to declare in 1861 for States’ rights, must bow her head
in shame.

It is true that several of the States have laws against
lynching, but they have proven to be grossly inadequate. It
is evident that other measures must be taken. Justice Brewer,
of the United States Supreme Court, one of the most learned
jurists of our time, has said: “Every man who takes part in
the burning or lynching of a negro is a murderer, and should
be so considered in the eyes of the law.” It is plain that one
of the causes of lynching is the race prejudice. There has
grown a feeling here in the South that a negro should be
lynched for certain crimes, while a white man, committing the
same crime, should not. Many cases of white men commit-
ting that terrible crime, too horrible to be mentioned, and not 
being lynched attest the truth of this statement. But this con-
dition of things should not be. Simply because a man is black
is no reason why he should not have some show of justice. Let
us adhere rather to the principle of our forefathers, “Equal
rights to all—privileges to none.” Every one must see that if
this mob violence is allowed to proceed unhindered, certain
results will appear in its wake. Human life will depreciate in
value. Mob violence is no more than a form of anarchy, and
anarchy is but the forerunner of tyranny. Our government
will go down in ruin.

But let us see what is the primary cause of lynching. In
the lower strata of society, whence almost invariably comes the
rapist, we find an amazing amount of ignorance concerning the
heinousness of this crime. No one believes it is wrong; but,
on the contrary, they believe it is both morally aud legally
right. There are thousands of illiterate negroes and whites in
this State who have no idia what a black crime is this. It is
not true, as is often asserted, that the criminal class of negroes
is drawn from the educated part of the race; but it is the igno-
rant negroes mainly who are criminals. Likewise, those who
compose the lynching mobs are generally of the ignorant class
of white men. It has been said that education is not a remedy
for every evil. This is true; we must concede that education
is the fundamental remedy for the evil of the mob violence.
There is another cause of lynching. Justice pursuing the
breaker of the law with the sword of punishment uplifted, is
retarded by the impediments of legal quibbles, delays, techni-
calities, red tape and other subterfuges too numerous to men-
tion. We must purify the methods in practice in our courts.
There was once a time when one could feel assured that the
law would give a criminal swift and speedy justice. But such
is not now the case. And, rather than undergo the uncertainty
of a trial, the friends of the injured one resort to summary
methods which are indeed deplorable.

There is the radical remedy of stern force as a last resort
in eradicating mob violence. It has been found effective in
Indiana, where the governor promptly called out the militia
and quelled the lynchers. If the executives of other States
would follow his example, instances of mob violence would be
reduced to a minimum.

Gentlemen, it is fitting that we should discuss this momen-
tous question. We are the coming men of the South. In
five years more we shall all have entered the arena of life.
Much will be expected of us. During the generation of our
fathers men have gone onward and upward in every sphere of
life. The philosopher has rushed into the holiest arcana of
philosophy and held up in triumph his speculations and his
theories. The inventor has made machines that seem to per-
form the work of the human mind. The natural scientist has
wrung from Mother Nature her closely locked and guarded
secrets. We are to take up the burden of life and go to higher
and better things. And let us rise up in the glorious strength
of our young manhood and wipe this stain from the name of
the South.
